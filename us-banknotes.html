US banknote serial numbers: what makes a note collectible

Almost every US note, or bill, is worth its face value. A few are worth more to collectors because of the serial number printed on them.

This page explains how US serials work and what collectors look for. Picave checks each note for you.

Covers Federal Reserve notes from Series 1963 to Series 2025, $1 to $100. Older types, such as silver certificates, are not covered.

Checking a pile of old notes

Check each one before spending or depositing it. Old notes can still be spent: the US Bureau of Engraving and Printing says all designs of US currency remain legal tender, regardless of when they were issued.

  1. Keep notes that were stored together in their original order. Consecutive serial numbers can form a run, which collectors look at as a set.
  2. Scan each note with Picave, or type its serial and choose its denomination. Picave shows the note's series and signatures, with badges for pattern serials and star notes.
  3. Grade the notes that stand out. Picave's grading guide asks a short set of questions about the note's condition, then shows whether the note is collectible at that grade.
  4. Look closely at any star note. Its scarcity depends on how many were printed in its run, which Picave shows where the printing records give it.

How US serial numbers work

Every note has a serial number of letters and eight digits. Where the letters sit depends on the note and the series.

$5 to $100, from Series 1996

PSeries LBank 41827396Number CSuffix

Two letters, eight digits and a letter, as on this made-up Series 2017A $20. The first letter is the series, and the second is the Federal Reserve Bank that issued the note, from A to L.

$1 and $2 notes, and older $5 to $100

LBank 41827396Number DSuffix

One letter, eight digits and a letter, as on this made-up $1. The first letter is the Federal Reserve Bank. There is no series letter, so the series has to be read from the front of the note, where it is printed.

Star notes

PL 41827396 *Star

A star in place of the last letter marks a star note, printed to replace a faulty note found during printing.

What collectors look for

Condition comes first. Picave's catalogue records the lowest grade at which each note attracts collectors, and for almost every US series that is Extremely Fine. Once a note is graded, Picave shows whether it is collectible at that grade, or the grade it would need.

Pattern serials

A serial whose eight digits form a pattern is the best-known kind of collectible. These examples are made up, using a real series and bank.

Radar

PL 35799753 C

Reads the same forwards and backwards.

Repeater

PL 41824182 C

The first half of the number repeats in the second half.

Solid

PL 77777777 C

Every digit is the same. Rare.

Ladder

PL 12345678 C

The digits climb or fall one step at a time.

Binary

PL 16611611 C

The number uses only two different digits.

Bookend

PL 45120045 C

Starts and ends with the same pair of digits.

Low serial

PL 00000007 C

A number from 1 to 100.

Picave checks the eight digits, and it also flags near patterns, one digit away from a radar, ladder or binary.

Star notes and their print runs

Star notes are printed in runs, and most come from full runs that are not scarce. Collectors look for star notes from short runs of 640,000 notes or fewer. Picave shows the size of a star note's run where the printing records give it, and treats only a short run as rare.

Series and signatures

Each series carries two signatures, and the pair changes over time: more than 25 pairs appear from Series 1963 to Series 2025. Picave shows the series and signatures of every note it identifies.

Why identification can be tricky

A serial number alone does not always point to one note. These are the cases that catch collectors out, and how Picave handles each.

Serials with no series letter

On $1 and $2 notes, and on older $5 to $100 notes, nothing in the serial says which series the note belongs to, so one serial can fit every series. Picave's scanner reads the series printed on the note to choose it, and otherwise asks which series is printed on yours.

Star notes that look ordinary

A star note shares its letters and number with an ordinary note; only the star differs. Picave reads the star, then looks up the run it came from, because the run, not the star, decides whether the note is scarce.

Questions

Are old US bills worth anything?

Most are worth their face value, and they can still be spent: the US Bureau of Engraving and Printing says all designs of US currency remain legal tender, regardless of when they were issued. A few are worth more to collectors, such as star notes from short print runs and pattern serials, especially in good condition. Check each one with Picave before spending or depositing it.

What is a star note?

A star note replaces a faulty note found during printing. Its serial ends in a star instead of a letter, as in PL 41827396 *. Most star notes come from full print runs and are not scarce. Collectors look for runs of 640,000 notes or fewer, and Picave shows the run size where the printing records give it.

What do the letters in a US serial number mean?

On $5 to $100 notes from Series 1996, the first letter is the series and the second is the Federal Reserve Bank that issued the note, from A to L. On $1 and $2 notes, and on older notes, the first letter is the bank and there is no series letter.

Is my $2 bill worth anything?

Almost always, it is worth $2. A $2 bill attracts collectors when it is a star note from a short run, has a pattern serial such as a radar, solid or ladder, or a very low number, and is in good condition. Scan it with Picave to see its series and any of these features.

What is a radar note?

A radar serial reads the same forwards and backwards, like 35799753. Picave checks the eight digits for radars and the other patterns collectors look for.

What is a bookend serial?

A bookend serial starts and ends with the same pair of digits, like 45120045. Collectors of US notes look for them, and Picave flags them on US notes.
