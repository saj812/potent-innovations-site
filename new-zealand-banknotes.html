New Zealand banknote serial numbers: what makes a note collectible

Almost every New Zealand note is worth its face value. A few are worth more to collectors because of the serial number printed on them.

This page explains how New Zealand serials work and what collectors look for. Picave checks each note for you.

Covers New Zealand's decimal notes from 1967 to 2022. Pre-decimal notes are not covered.

Checking a pile of old notes

Check each one before banking or spending it. Old notes can still be exchanged: the Reserve Bank of New Zealand says it will always pay face value for currency legally issued for use in New Zealand, even once it is no longer legal tender.

  1. Keep notes that were stored together in their original order. Consecutive serial numbers can form a run, which collectors look at as a set.
  2. Scan each note with Picave, or type its serial and choose its denomination. Picave shows when the note was printed and whose signature it carries, with badges for pattern serials, first and last prefixes and star notes.
  3. Grade the notes that stand out. Picave's grading guide asks a short set of questions about the note's condition, with separate questions for paper and polymer, then shows whether the note is collectible at that grade.
  4. Show a specialist any note Picave marks as a Collector Issue. These have features that need hands-on expertise to confirm.

How New Zealand serial numbers work

Every note has a serial number made of a prefix and six digits. The prefix changed shape over the years, so how a note was printed shows in it.

Paper notes, 1967 to 1999

TBKPrefix 418273Number

A prefix of up to three characters, then six digits, as on this made-up $20 from the early 1980s. Paper prefixes can be letters, numbers or a mix of both.

Paper notes carry no year, and many prefixes were printed over several years under one signature. Picave shows that span of years, such as 1981 to 1985, rather than guessing a single year.

Polymer notes, from 1999

CKPrefix 16Year 418273Number

Two letters and two digits, then six more, as on this made-up 2016 $20. The two digits after the letters are the year of printing: 16 means 2016.

Star notes

A1*Prefix and star 274615Number

On a New Zealand star note, the star sits between the prefix and the six digits, as on this made-up paper $10.

What collectors look for

Condition comes first. Picave's catalogue records the lowest grade at which each note attracts collectors, and for many New Zealand notes that is Extremely Fine. Once a note is graded, Picave shows whether it is collectible at that grade, or the grade it would need.

Pattern serials

A serial whose six digits form a pattern is the best-known kind of collectible. These examples are made up, using a real prefix and year.

Radar

CK16 357753

Reads the same forwards and backwards.

Repeater

CK16 418418

The first half of the number repeats in the second half.

Solid

CK16 777777

Every digit is the same. Rare.

Ladder

CK16 123456

The digits climb or fall one step at a time.

Binary

CK16 616161

The number uses only two different digits.

Low serial

AA16 000007

A number from 1 to 100.

Picave checks the six digits after the prefix, and it also flags near patterns, one digit away from a radar, ladder or binary. Bookends (a number that starts and ends with the same pair) are collected on US notes but are not counted as a pattern on New Zealand notes.

First and last prefixes

Each run of notes starts at a first prefix and ends at a last prefix, and collectors look for both. They differ from note to note and from one signature to the next. Picave marks them with a First Prefix or Last Prefix badge.

Star notes

On paper notes, faulty notes found during printing were replaced with star notes. Picave knows which prefixes were used for star notes, marks them with a Star Note badge and treats them as rare.

Signatures

New Zealand notes carry a single signature, and ten different signatures appear across the decimal notes. Where a serial fits notes with different signatures, Picave shows each one with an image of the signature to compare with your note.

Notes that need a specialist

A few notes, including special issues and some replacement notes, have details a serial number cannot settle. Picave marks these as a Collector Issue and suggests a specialist.

Why identification can be tricky

A serial number alone does not always point to one note. These are the cases that catch collectors out, and how Picave handles each.

A star that goes missing

Take the star out of a star note's serial, through a scan that misses it or a typing slip, and what is left is still a valid serial for a different note. When a serial could be either, Picave offers both, so you can check your note for the star.

One prefix, more than one signature

Some prefixes carried on from one signature to the next. When a serial fits more than one note, Picave shows the candidates with their signatures, so you can pick the one on your note.

Paper notes with no single year

Paper notes do not carry a year, and many prefixes were printed over several years. Picave shows the span of years for the note's signature instead of picking one. On polymer notes, the year in the prefix settles it.

Questions

Are old New Zealand banknotes worth anything?

Most are worth their face value, and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand says it will always pay face value for currency legally issued for use in New Zealand, even once it is no longer legal tender. A few are worth more to collectors, such as star notes, first and last prefixes and pattern serials, especially in good condition. Check each one with Picave before banking or spending it, and keep notes found together in their original order.

Is my $20 note worth anything?

Almost always, it is worth $20. A $20 note attracts collectors when its serial is a star note, a first or last prefix, a pattern such as a radar, solid or ladder, or a very low number, and it is in good condition. Scan it with Picave to see when it was printed, its signature and any of these features.

What is a star note in New Zealand?

A star note is a replacement note, printed to take the place of a faulty note found during production. On New Zealand paper notes the star sits between the prefix and the six digits, as in A1* 274615.

What year was my note printed?

On polymer notes, the two digits after the letters are the year of printing: CK16 means 2016. Paper notes carry no year, and many prefixes were printed over several years, so Picave shows the span of years for the note's signature.

What is a radar note?

A radar serial reads the same forwards and backwards, like 357753. Picave checks the six digits after the prefix for radars and the other patterns collectors look for.

Why does Picave show two possible notes for my serial?

Either the prefix was used under more than one signature, or the serial could belong to a star note whose star was missed. Picave shows both notes so you can match the signature, or check your note for a star.
