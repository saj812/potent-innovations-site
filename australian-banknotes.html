Australian banknote serial numbers: what makes a note collectible

Almost every Australian note is worth its face value. A few are worth more to collectors because of the serial number printed on them.

This page explains how Australian serials work and what collectors look for. Picave checks each note for you.

Covers Australia's decimal notes from 1966 to 2023. Pre-decimal notes are not covered.

Checking a pile of old notes

Check each one before banking or spending it. Old notes can still be banked: the Reserve Bank of Australia says all previous issues of Australian banknotes remain legal tender, and it and most banks will redeem old notes at face value.

  1. Keep notes that were stored together in their original order. Consecutive serial numbers can form a run, which collectors look at as a set.
  2. Scan each note with Picave, or type its serial and choose its denomination. Picave shows the note's year and signatures, with badges for pattern serials, first and last prefixes, low print runs, star notes and test notes.
  3. Grade the notes that stand out. Picave's grading guide asks a short set of questions about the note's condition, with separate questions for paper and polymer, then shows whether the note is collectible at that grade.
  4. Show a specialist any note Picave marks as a Collector Issue. These have features that need hands-on expertise to confirm.

How Australian serial numbers work

Every note has a serial number made of a prefix (the letters) and a number. How they are laid out depends on when the note was printed.

Paper notes, 1966 to 1993

TCKPrefix 418273Number

Three letters and six digits, as on this made-up 1974 $10. Paper notes carry no year, so the year comes from the prefix and the two signatures.

Polymer notes, from 1988

HFPrefix 09Year 274615Number

Two letters and eight digits, as on this made-up 2009 $50. The first two digits are the year of printing: 09 means 2009. The Reserve Bank of Australia describes the two letters as the note's position on the printing sheet.

The first polymer notes are the exception. The 1988 $10 and the 1992 $5 use plain running numbers, so their first two digits are not a year.

The newer designs, from 2016

DBPrefix 19Year 2846150Number

Two letters and nine digits, as on this made-up 2019 $20. The first two digits are still the year, and the other seven are the note's number within that year's run. Nine-digit serials arrived with the newer designs, which the Reserve Bank began issuing on 1 September 2016.

What collectors look for

Condition comes first. Picave's catalogue records the lowest grade at which each note attracts collectors, and for most notes that is Very Fine or better. Once a note is graded, Picave shows whether it is collectible at that grade, or the grade it would need.

Pattern serials

A serial whose digits form a pattern is the best-known kind of collectible. These examples are made up, using real prefixes and years.

Radar

HF09357753

Reads the same forwards and backwards.

Repeater

HF09418418

The first half of the number repeats in the second half.

Solid

HF09777777

Every digit is the same. Rare.

Ladder

DB191234567

The digits climb or fall one step at a time.

Binary

HF09616161

The number uses only two different digits.

Low serial

AA190000007

A number from 1 to 100 within its run.

On polymer notes whose first two digits are a year, collectors look at the digits after the year. Picave checks both the whole number and the digits after the year, and it also flags near patterns, one digit away from a radar, ladder or binary. Bookends (a number that starts and ends with the same pair) are collected on US notes but are not counted as a pattern on Australian notes.

First and last prefixes

Each run of notes starts at a first prefix and ends at a last prefix, and collectors look for both. They differ from year to year and note to note. Picave marks them with a First Prefix or Last Prefix badge.

Short print runs

Some prefixes were printed in far smaller numbers than usual. Picave marks a prefix printed in a run of fewer than 640,000 notes with a Low Print Run badge.

Star notes

On early paper notes, faulty notes found during printing were replaced with star notes. Their prefix starts with Z, and a star takes the place of the last digit. Picave knows which prefixes were used for star notes, marks them with a Star Note badge and treats them as rare.

ZFCPrefix 27183*Number and star

Signatures

Since 1966, Australian notes have carried more than 20 different signature pairs. On paper notes, with no year printed, the signatures are often the only way to tell two notes with the same prefix apart. Picave shows the signatures of every note it identifies.

Test notes

A few polymer notes were printed as trials, and ordinary notes share some of their prefixes. Picave marks the prefixes where test notes are known with a Test Note badge, and a specialist should confirm one.

Notes that need a specialist

Some notes changed part way through a run in ways a serial number cannot show. Picave marks these as a Collector Issue and suggests a specialist, such as an ANDA-member dealer. They include:

  • paper notes printed in two serial fonts, Gothic and OCR-B
  • notes with the security thread in different positions
  • notes printed with and without plate letters
  • early polymer $5 notes printed with different shades of serial ink

Why identification can be tricky

A serial number alone does not always point to one note. These are the cases that catch collectors out, and how Picave handles each.

One prefix, two signature pairs

Many prefixes were used again after the signatures changed: in Picave's catalogue, more than 80 prefixes appear with two different signature pairs. When a serial fits more than one note, Picave shows the candidates with their signatures, so you can pick the one on your note.

A change part way through a run

Sometimes the signatures changed within a single prefix, so the number decides which pair a note carries. Picave tells them apart by the number.

The same prefix in two years

Some paper prefixes were printed in both 1972 and 1974 with the same signatures. The 1972 notes read COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA across the top, and the 1974 notes read AUSTRALIA. Picave's scanner reads that title to choose the year, and otherwise asks which one you have.

Running numbers that look like a year

The first polymer $5 uses plain running numbers, so a 1992 note can look like a later year: AA 01 375940 could be a 1992 or a 2001 $5. Picave shows both, with their signatures.

Questions

Are old Australian banknotes worth anything?

Most are worth their face value, and they can still be used: the Reserve Bank of Australia says all previous issues of Australian banknotes remain legal tender. A few are worth more to collectors, such as first and last prefixes, star notes, pattern serials and notes from short print runs. Check each one with Picave before banking or spending it, and keep notes found together in their original order.

Is my $20 note worth anything?

Almost always, it is worth $20. A $20 note attracts collectors when its serial is a first or last prefix, a pattern such as a radar, solid or ladder, or a very low number, or when it comes from a short print run, and it is in good condition. Scan it with Picave to see its year, signatures and any of these features.

What is a star note in Australia?

A star note is a replacement note, printed to take the place of a faulty note found during production. Australian star notes are early paper notes. Their prefix starts with Z, and a star takes the place of the last digit, as in ZFC 27183*.

What does AA at the start of a serial mean?

On polymer notes, the Reserve Bank of Australia says the two letters show the note's position on the printing sheet. AA is usually the first prefix of a year's run, but not for every note. Picave shows whether a note is a first or last prefix.

What year was my note printed?

On polymer notes, the first two digits after the letters are the year of printing: 09 means 2009. The 1988 $10 and the 1992 $5 are exceptions, numbered in plain sequence. Paper notes carry no year at all, so the year comes from the prefix and the signatures, and a few prefixes were printed in two different years.

What is a radar note?

A radar serial reads the same forwards and backwards, like 357753. On polymer notes whose first two digits are a year, collectors look at the digits after the year, so HF 09 357753 counts as a radar. Picave checks both the whole number and the digits after the year.

Why do notes with the same prefix have different signatures?

The same prefix was often used again after the signatures changed, and sometimes the change came part way through a run. When a serial fits more than one note, Picave shows the signatures so you can pick the one on your note.
