UK banknote serial numbers: what makes a note collectible

Almost every Bank of England note is worth its face value. A few are worth more to collectors because of the serial number printed on them.

This page explains how Bank of England serials work and what collectors look for. Picave checks each note for you.

Covers Bank of England notes from Series D (1970) to today's polymer notes. Notes issued by Scottish and Northern Irish banks are not covered.

Checking a pile of old notes

Check each one before spending or exchanging it. Old paper notes can still be exchanged: the Bank of England says there is no deadline to exchange old banknotes with it by post, and the Post Office accepts most old notes as a deposit into a UK bank account.

  1. Keep notes that were stored together in their original order. Consecutive serial numbers can form a run, which collectors look at as a set.
  2. Scan each note with Picave, or type its serial and choose its denomination. Picave shows the note's design and Chief Cashier, with badges for pattern serials, first and last prefixes and replacement notes.
  3. Grade the notes that stand out. Picave's grading guide asks a short set of questions about the note's condition, with separate questions for paper and polymer, then shows whether the note is collectible at that grade.
  4. Show a specialist any note Picave marks as a Collector Issue. These have features that need hands-on expertise to confirm.

How Bank of England serial numbers work

Every note has a prefix and a six-digit number. The prefix takes one of three forms, depending on the design.

Polymer notes, and most paper notes

CB12Prefix 418273Number

Two letters and two digits, then six digits, as on this made-up £20.

Older paper designs

E01Prefix 418273Number

Some older designs used a single letter and two digits, as on this made-up £50. The £20 of the 1970s and 1980s put two digits before the letter instead, as in 05L 418273.

No date on the note

Bank of England notes carry no date. The Chief Cashier's signature and the design show when a note was printed, and Picave shows the years that Chief Cashier signed, such as 1994 to 1998.

What collectors look for

Condition comes first. Picave's catalogue records the lowest grade at which each note attracts collectors, and for most Bank of England notes that is Extremely Fine. Once a note is graded, Picave shows whether it is collectible at that grade, or the grade it would need.

Pattern serials

A serial whose six digits form a pattern is the best-known kind of collectible. These examples are made up, using a real prefix.

Radar

CB12 357753

Reads the same forwards and backwards.

Repeater

CB12 418418

The first half of the number repeats in the second half.

Solid

CB12 777777

Every digit is the same. Rare.

Ladder

CB12 123456

The digits climb or fall one step at a time.

Binary

CB12 616161

The number uses only two different digits.

Low serial

AA01 000007

A number from 1 to 100.

Picave checks the six digits after the prefix, and it also flags near patterns, one digit away from a radar, ladder or binary. Bookends are collected on US notes but are not counted as a pattern on Bank of England notes.

AA01 and other first prefixes

The first notes of recent designs carry the prefix AA01, and collectors look for them, along with the first and last prefixes of each Chief Cashier's run. Picave marks these with a First Prefix or Last Prefix badge.

Replacement notes

Some paper series used special prefixes, such as LL, for notes that replaced faulty ones found during printing. Picave knows these prefixes, marks the notes with a Star Note badge and treats them as rare.

Designs and Chief Cashiers

Each design is known by the figure on the back, such as Turner on the polymer £20, and each note carries the signature of the Chief Cashier who signed it. Ten Chief Cashiers appear from Series D to today. Picave shows the design and signature of every note it identifies.

Notes that need a specialist

A few early notes have details a serial number cannot settle. Picave marks these as a Collector Issue and suggests a specialist.

Why identification can be tricky

A serial number alone does not always point to one note. These are the cases that catch collectors out, and how Picave handles each.

One prefix, several designs

Prefixes were used again across designs, on paper and on polymer, so one serial can fit several notes. Picave shows the options with their design and signature, and tells you what separates them: whether the note is paper or polymer, the picture on the back, or the signature.

Two Chief Cashiers on one prefix

When a new Chief Cashier took over, the new signature sometimes appeared on prefixes the previous one had used, so the same prefix carries both signatures. Picave shows both, so you can compare the signature on your note.

No printed date

With no date on the note, the year is never certain from the serial alone. Picave shows the span of years the Chief Cashier signed, rather than guessing a single year.

Questions

Are old paper £20 and £50 notes worth anything?

They can still be exchanged at face value: the Bank of England says there is no deadline to exchange old banknotes with it by post, and the Post Office accepts most old notes as a deposit into a UK bank account. A few are worth more to collectors, such as first prefixes, replacement notes and pattern serials, especially in good condition. Check each one with Picave first.

What is an AA01 note?

On recent Bank of England designs, the first notes printed carry the prefix AA01. Collectors look for them, especially with a low number. Picave marks first prefixes with a badge.

What is a replacement note?

A replacement note takes the place of a faulty note found during printing. Some paper series used special prefixes for them, such as LL, as in LL12 418273. Picave knows these prefixes and treats the notes as rare.

When was my note printed?

Bank of England notes carry no date. The design and the Chief Cashier's signature show the era, and Picave shows the years that Chief Cashier signed.

What is a radar note?

A radar serial reads the same forwards and backwards, like 357753. Picave checks the six digits after the prefix for radars and the other patterns collectors look for.

Why does Picave show more than one possible note?

Prefixes were used again across designs and Chief Cashiers, so one serial can fit several notes. Picave shows each option and tells you what to check on your note: paper or polymer, the picture on the back, or the signature.
